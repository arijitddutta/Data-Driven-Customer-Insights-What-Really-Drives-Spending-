  1. Does education level impact average monthly spend? (One-way ANOVA)
  • H₀: There is no significant difference in average MonthlySpend across different Education levels
  • H₁: At least one Education level has a significantly different average MonthlySpend
  1. Are older people less active? (Correlation - Age vs DaysSinceLastInteraction)
  • H₀: There is no significant correlation between Age and DaysSinceLastInteraction
  • H₁: There is a significant correlation between Age and DaysSinceLastInteraction
  1. Does state-wise spend vary significantly? (ANOVA)
  • H₀: There is no significant difference in average MonthlySpend across different States
  • H₁: At least one State has a significantly different average MonthlySpend

Phase 7 — Testing The Hypothesis¶

Do males and females spend differently? (Independent t-test) :¶

  • H₀: There is no significant difference in average MonthlySpend between Male and Female customers
  • H₁: There is a significant difference in average MonthlySpend between Male and Female customers
  • Significance Level : 0.05(α)

from scipy import stats

male_spend = df[df['Gender'] == 'Male']['MonthlySpend']
female_spend = df[df['Gender'] == 'Female']['MonthlySpend']

t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(male_spend, female_spend)
print("T-statistic:", t_stat)
print("P-value:", p_value)
alpha = 0.05
if p_value < alpha:
    print("Reject the Null Hypothesis")
else:
    print("Fail to Reject the Null Hypothesis")

Observation :¶

Independent T-Test (Gender vs MonthlySpend)

  • Performed stats.ttest_ind() on Male and Female MonthlySpend at α = 0.05
  • Results: T-statistic = 0.339, P-value = 0.734
  • Since p-value (0.734) > α (0.05), we fail to reject the null hypothesis
  • Conclusion: No statistically significant difference in MonthlySpend between Male and Female customers.

Key Business Insights¶

Key findings from the customer data analysis

Demographic Independence of Spending

  • One of the biggest takeaways is that traditional demographics just don't predict how much people spend. Whether we’re looking at gender, education, or state, the spending patterns are effectively the same across the board (all with p-values > 0.05). If we’ve been spending time trying to segment our pricing or marketing based on these traits, we’re likely not getting much return on that effort.

  • Focusing on the Big Spenders While most of our customers fall in the $100–$300 range, we have a consistent group of "power users" who spend significantly more. The catch is that they aren't tied to any specific demographic—they’re scattered everywhere. Instead of segmenting by background, I think we’d get much better results by identifying these high-value individuals and building a specific strategy to keep them engaged.

Age isn't a factor

  • I looked into whether age affects how much people spend or how often they check in with us, and the numbers are essentially flat (-0.012 and -0.004). We should probably stop using age as a filter for our engagement strategies; it’s just not telling us anything useful about how they behave.

Engagement Variability and Future Research

  • The biggest question mark in the data is why some customers haven't interacted with us in a long time. The variance here is massive (an average swing of about 399 days), and since demographics don't explain it, we’re looking at something else—maybe the product type, support history, or the channel they came in through. We need to look deeper into the service or product side of things to figure out why we’re losing touch.

Business Impact

  • Overall, our customer base is pretty healthy and balanced. We don’t have one group dominating the data, though we do have a slight tilt toward Florida/California residents and those with Master’s degrees. It’s a diverse group, which actually gives us a lot of room to experiment with new strategies that don’t rely on old-school demographics
